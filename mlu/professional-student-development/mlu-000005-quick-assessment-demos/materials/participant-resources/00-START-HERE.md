*Monday, September 21, 2026 · 2026 AWS-MLU AI Teaching & Research Symposium, Howard University*

You spent 90 minutes watching one assignment travel through four AI tools — building its rubric,
hardening it against chatbots, grading it, and letting students check their own drafts first. You
did three of those four with your hands on the keyboard.

**This pack lets you do it again, on your own course, at your own pace.** You do not need to

## Putting Triage in front of students

This is the step that needs a conversation before it needs a tool.

Triage is the only one of the four that students touch directly. Before you point a class at it,
two things have to be true: the app has to be shared with people outside the session, and your
institution has to be comfortable with students' drafts going into it.

«FILL IN: What is actually permitted here? Who approves it — department chair, CIO, provost's
office? Is there an existing process? If the honest answer is "not yet, talk to us first," write
that. A clear no is useful; an unanswered question gets resolved by whoever is most optimistic.»

Note what Triage deliberately cannot do, because it is what makes the conversation easier: it
never returns a score, a tier, or a grade, and it never rewrites a student's words. It reports
Present, Partial, or Missing against your rubric, with the passage each status rests on. It cannot
compute a grade because it is never given the weights. Slide 23 covers the limits.

## The rubric on your handout

The paper rubric you graded Marcus Lee with at the start of the session is **the "before" example.**
It is deliberately weak, and it is not a model to copy.

If you kept that sheet: 60 of its 100 points go to things anyone can see without expertise —
organization, mechanics, length. "Understanding" is never defined. Nothing in it asks whether a
single claim in the paper is true. That is why Marcus scored 88 with three factually wrong claims
in a two-page paper.

Slides 9 through 12 are the whole argument. The line worth carrying out of the session is on
slide 9: **automation doesn't fix a rubric, it scales it.**

## The rules, which matter more when you are working alone

These were slide 27. They are the part of the session most worth keeping:

- **Draft, not verdict.** These tools prepare. You decide. Nothing here should record a grade
  without you reading it first.
- **Student work is a protected record.** Everything in the session was synthetic. Real student
  work goes only into systems your institution has approved — check before you paste, not after.
- **Not an integrity tool.** Nothing here detects plagiarism or AI-written text, and the
  AI-Proofing Assistant is not an AI detector despite the name. It hardens an assignment; it does
  not catch anyone.
- **You grade. Always.**

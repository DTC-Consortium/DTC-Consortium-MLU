# Grading rubric — Module 4 Final Project

> **This is not the author's rubric.** The original FAL 100 rubric lives inside Talladega College's
> Canvas instance and was not released with this contribution. The consortium assembled this
> substitute from the author's own project template and syllabus so that the final project is
> gradeable by adopters standalone. Replace it with the author's rubric if and when it is published.

Total **50 points**, matching the point value stated in the
[final project template](../assignments/final-project-template.md). Module 4 is worth **10% of the
course grade** per the syllabus, so the 50 points scale to that 10% in your gradebook.

| Part | Component | Points | What we're looking for |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1** | Major and career pathway | 5 | A specific major and one named career pathway, not a field-wide gesture. "Criminal Justice → probation officer" earns it; "Criminal Justice → jobs in justice" does not |
| **2** | Structured prompt | 15 | All five framework elements — Role, Context, Task, Format, Constraints — present and non-empty, each doing real work for *this* student's pathway |
| **3** | Evidence from all three tools | 10 | Separately identifiable output from PartyRock, ChatGPT, **and** Copilot, by paste, screenshot, or attached export |
| **4** | Comparison chart | 10 | All nine cells complete, each judgment pointing at something in the student's own outputs |
| **5** | Reflection questions | 10 | All five answered in complete sentences, grounded in what the student actually got back |
| | **Total** | **50** | |

The five parts map onto the syllabus outcomes this project is meant to evidence: *write effective
prompts using a structured prompting framework* (Part 2), *compare outputs generated by multiple AI
models* (Parts 3–4), *identify workplace applications of AI across multiple industries* (Part 1),
and *explain why AI literacy is becoming essential across every career field* (Part 5).

## Notes on scoring

**Part 2 carries the most weight, and the sample prompt is its biggest risk.** The template prints a
complete worked example — the Criminal Justice career coach — directly above the blank fields.
Expect submissions that swap the major and change nothing else. A prompt whose Role, Format, and
Constraints are the sample's verbatim has not demonstrated the framework; it has demonstrated
copy-paste. Award partial credit for the elements the student genuinely rewrote, and say this out
loud when you assign the work. It changes what you get back.

**Part 3 is binary per tool, not per section.** The common failure is one screenshot offered as
evidence for all three tools, or three outputs that are plainly the same text. Look for three
distinguishable responses. Roughly 3 / 3 / 4 points across PartyRock, ChatGPT, and Copilot works
cleanly, and a student who genuinely could not access one tool should say so in the document rather
than pad it — note it and grade the other two.

**A comparison chart of single words is not a comparison.** "Good / good / good" across nine cells
is the default submission if you let it be. The cell should say what made one output clearer or
better organized than another — "Copilot's table had salary but no job outlook" is the bar. This is
the part where the comparison outcome is actually assessed, so hold it.

**Reflections must cite the student's own results.** Question 2 asks what appeared in all three
responses and question 3 asks what differed; both are unanswerable in the abstract. A reflection
made of generic AI-is-transformative or AI-is-dangerous talking points, however fluently written,
has not met the bar. Question 5 is the exception — it invites speculation, and speculation is fine
there as long as it connects to the pathway named in Part 1.

**On responsible-use observations.** The syllabus lists recognizing hallucinations, bias, and
privacy risk among the course outcomes, and students often surface these unprompted in question 4
("what surprised you most") — an invented salary figure, a fabricated credential, a suggestion
skewed by an assumption about them. Credit it within Part 5 when it appears. This project does not
require it, so do not deduct for its absence.
